Jev Survey

Every answer type-checks. The evidence decides the rest.

TypeSafe’s Jev returns typed answers with a probability for every option, and open projects now copy its shape. This survey reads the studies, audits the open resources and traces every number to its source: what the probabilities mean, when software should act on them, and where typed decisions fail.

02 The object

A function call that returns a distribution

Jev is a hosted model from TypeSafe AI, released on 15 September 2026 as its first “System One” model. Software sends a text state and typed questions; the model answers from the declared options with probabilities and never writes free text.

choice

Choice

Returns
The chosen option, a probability for every option and a confidence value.
Semantics
A distribution over named options, each defined by a description. The launch post states support for up to 255 options.
Watch
The model reads option names and descriptions; a short label can outweigh its definition, and the vendor notes a lean toward the first option. F1 · Valid ≠ correct
Documentation ↗
score

Score

Returns
A position on ordered levels (it may fall between levels), probabilities per level and confidence.
Semantics
Two to ten described levels. Ordinal, not a regression: the vendor warns against interpolating exact magnitudes.
Watch
Middle levels can absorb errors, and decisions can use only part of the scale. F1 · Valid ≠ correct
Documentation ↗
noul

Noul

Returns
The probability that the answer is yes. No separate confidence field.
Semantics
A proposition probability, optionally with descriptions of what counts as true and false.
Watch
A yes/no question and a two-option Choice over the same proposition need not agree; a statement and its negation need not sum to one. F8 · Calibrated ≠ coherent
Documentation ↗

The commercial model

TypeSafe’s hosted Jev, version jev-1.13.0. Its interface, prices, limits and known weak spots are documented; its architecture, training data and confidence statistic are not.

Jev-like typed decision models: encoders, encoder–decoders, frozen or fine-tuned decoders, diffusion reads and adapters that reproduce the request and response shape with other mechanisms.

The systems

Judges, agents, search engines, databases, robots and annotation pipelines whose results depend on typed decisions — and on much else.

Six research questions

RQ1

Definition & lineage

How do runtime-defined finite-option decisions relate to classifiers, entailment-based labelling, rerankers, reward models and constrained generation?

RQ2

Probability quality

What do returned probabilities and confidence mean, for which tasks, languages and distributions, and are they coherent across related questions?

RQ3

Efficiency attribution

How much of a speed or cost gain comes from the readout, and how much from model size, batching, prefix sharing, caching, pruning or the network?

RQ4

Selective control

Which decisions can be delegated, and when should software escalate, abstain or let code compute — judged by end outcome and total cost?

RQ5

Robustness & security

How do option names, order, definitions, missing options, language, injected text and poisoned checkpoints change decisions?

RQ6

Openness & reproducibility

What do alternatives release — code, weights, data, predictions — and how much of the literature survives a common protocol?

F1 Valid ≠ correct · Decision contract

A type-valid answer can still be the wrong answer.

99% → 7%

right when the answer is on the menu; correct rejections when it is missing, an explicit “None” notwithstanding

Rejection Bottleneck

Typed interfaces remove parse and schema failures by construction. They do not ensure that the model reads the options the way the author meant: short labels can outweigh written definitions, rebinding names to rubrics can reverse a ranking, a middle option can absorb errors, and when the right answer is missing the model usually picks a wrong option instead of the exit. Order effects can change many individual answers while aggregate accuracy stays flat. The type-error rate stays at zero throughout.

F2 Measurable ≠ calibrated · Probability & calibration

A native probability makes calibration measurable, not guaranteed.

+0.21–0.33

confidence above accuracy on news past the model’s knowledge boundary, even after recalibration

Beyond Answer Confidence

Every call returns a distribution, so reliability can be audited on each run. Measured calibration varies by task, construct and language; probabilities can rank well yet sit badly against a fixed threshold; temperatures fitted on a few options transfer poorly to many; and confidence stays high where the model lacks the knowledge. Thresholds and recalibration fitted on separate data help, but only for the workload they were fitted on.

JEV-as-a-Judge

Across judging, annotation, moderation, intrusion detection, infrastructure triage and agents, the typed model is usually cheaper and faster but not the most accurate component. Gains appear when uncertain cases go to a stronger model or a person and thresholds are frozen on separate data; the strongest designs fix them in advance and test on new workloads. Savings shrink once pre-screen costs, held-out misses and cheap trained classifiers are counted, so those belong in the comparison.

F4 Fast is not one number · Downstream use

Speed and cost claims decompose into different mechanisms and scopes.

1.2–3.3×

the input tokens billed per decision against a batch-priced LLM, leaving no cost advantage

Political Science Replications

Single-request latency, per-question amortized time, batch throughput, end-to-end task time, deadline attainment under load, simulated timing and API bills are different quantities. Prefix sharing, batching, caching and pruning can explain as much as the readout does; billing every option as input can erase the price advantage against batch-priced LLMs; and a faster step can make a slower agent. None of the figures can be pooled into a single leaderboard.

A Spanish state with English instructions lost accuracy on every tested dataset, calibration worsened on the hardest tasks, and persona shifts reproduced a smaller share of human differences in Arabic than in English.

Probability & calibration

Two-decimal probabilities

Probabilities are returned on a 0.01 grid, which puts a floor under calibration error for rare events and can place exactly zero on a correct answer.

Mitigation Treat rare-event probabilities with care; smooth before log-loss.

  • Calibrated Decisions at ScaleAuthor-reported experiment
  • scienthoon/jev-ood-calibration ↗Community report (repository)

Selective control

Thresholds that do not travel

A threshold set for 5% in-scope risk still admitted 31% of out-of-scope requests, a rejection threshold fitted on one task rejected 69% of covered inputs on another, binary probabilities ranked well but sat badly against 0.5, and in-sample thresholds aimed at 5% misses gave up to 17% on held-out data.

Mitigation Fit thresholds on held-out data from the deployment distribution, per option count, and re-check after any version change.

07 The method atlas

Six ways to return a typed answer

Implementations share the request and response shape, not the mechanism behind the probabilities. Select a family to see how it works, what is public and where its evidence stops.

A · Hosted System One service

  1. InputText state + typed questions
  2. BackboneUndisclosed hosted model
  3. ReadoutUndisclosed; distribution over the declared options
  4. OutputAnswer + probabilities + confidence

Hosted System One service

A closed, hosted model returns a distribution over declared options for each typed question about a shared text state.

What is public
Interface, primitives, pricing, limits, versioning and known failure modes are documented; architecture, training data and the exact confidence statistic are not.
Where evidence stops
Black-box evaluation only; the served model can change behind aliases; text-only input.
Representatives
  • TypeSafe Jev (jev-1.13.0)

Studies in this family

B · Encoder decision heads

  1. InputState ⊕ question ⊕ options
  2. BackboneBidirectional encoder or encoder–decoder
  3. ReadoutPer-option score → softmax over the legal set
  4. OutputDistribution over options

Encoder decision heads

A bidirectional encoder, or an encoder–decoder, reads state, question and options; a trained head scores each option and a softmax is taken over the legal set.

What is public
Weights and inference code for most members; several release training code; the readout geometry (marker token, span mean, blind per-option scoring) changes name and order sensitivity.
Where evidence stops
Short context windows; option labels can dominate written definitions; adjacent to GLiClass, SetFit and NLI zero-shot classifiers.
Representatives
  • Laya (ModernBERT-large, mmBERT-base)
  • Bongard (T5Gemma 2 encoder–decoder, 2609.39111)
  • language-specific encoders: Chinese-Jev, sokudan (Japanese), ufakzeka-karar (Turkish)
  • SBERT2S1 (biomedical sentence encoders)
  • WaterSheep, Verdict (ModernBERT)

C · Frozen decoder option readout

  1. InputPrompt listing options with short identifiers
  2. BackboneFrozen causal LM
  3. ReadoutNext-token logits of the identifiers, renormalised; optional debiasing
  4. OutputDistribution over options

Frozen decoder option readout

An unmodified causal LM is prompted once and the next-token logits of short option identifiers are renormalised over the declared options, optionally debiased over label priors and option rotations.

What is public
Fully open and training-free; a frozen 4B model read this way matches community fine-tunes on public JevBench items — the natural baseline for any trained decision model.
Where evidence stops
Raw logits are order- and label-sensitive; one prefill cannot reason; rotation averaging multiplies cost by the number of rotations.

  1. CoreBOTTLED

    Agent in a Bottle: Can LLM Agents Turn Their Capabilities Into Cheap, Scalable Artifacts?

    Ankit Sonthalia, Haritz Puerto, Alexander Rubinstein et al.

    Strong zero-shot models did not reliably bottle (48 of 60 runs fell below their model’s zero-shot interval), but bottling could be far cheaper; against zero-shot Jev on 1,000-item samples, an Opus 5 solution reached 94% of Jev’s ESCI macro-F1 (0.499 against 0.531) at about a quarter of Jev’s projected full-workload cost, and a Gemini 3.1 Pro solution 97% of Jev’s RAID AUROC (0.822 against 0.846) at about 2% of its cost.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code

  2. CoreSystem One Moderation

    Benchmarking System One Models in Online Moderation

    Federico Mazzoni, Andrea Failla

    With the full rule inventory held fixed, retrieved precedents raised Jev’s exact policy-selection accuracy by 12.35, 5.20 and 22.20 points on three evaluations while Laya gained little or lost; precedents raised Jev’s AUROC on OLID (.789 → .816), HateXplain (.749 → .755) and ModerationBench (.893 → .936 with Choice), and its confidence supported selective review (71.9% of ModerationBench decisions at confidence ≥ 0.8, with 94.3% accuracy), although it was not consistently calibrated.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code

  3. CoreReadout Stability

    Readout Stability in Prefill-Only Decision Models:Zero-Label Prediction and Inference-Time Compute Allocation

    Ran Li, Lei Chen

    Across seven open decision-model families, accuracy after a menu-only change was predicted from the first pass within 4.2 points (exactly for one family), while same-scale generative models erred by 1.6–15.8 points; extra order-permuted passes cut calibration error by 67% but added almost no accuracy, a confidence cascade to a larger sibling beat every re-ask scheme at matched cost, and a 0.8B model on a curated five-candidate menu reached 95.4% on CLINC150 against 80.0% for a 4B model on all 150 labels.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code

  4. CoreSanSi

    SanSi: A Looped Typed Decision Model for System 1.5 Thinking

    Shuyu Gan, Young-Jun Lee, Dongyeop Kang

    Eight loops raised accuracy from 58.4% to 72.0% at fixed shape, data and recipe (+13.5 points), within 1.8 points of a single-pass model three times larger, and extended solvable reasoning depth beyond training; hosted Jev, not trained on these data, scored 78.9% overall but gave a hard answer to 62.8% of items whose deciding evidence had been removed and fell from 100% to chance on liar chains as the number of steps grew.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code not located

  5. CoreS1-MAS

    Token-Efficient Multi-Agent Collaboration via System One-Guided Computational Division of Labor

    Zihan Zhou, Xinzhe Hu, Hanxu Yang et al.

    S1-MAS reached the highest mean accuracy on six benchmarks (macro-average 93.97%, 0.85 points above DyLAN) while cutting GPT-4o tokens by 78.3–94.0% and latency by 58.2–91.7% against five multi-agent baselines; in ablation, Laya’s coordination decisions beat random choices from the same legal menus by only 1.83 points but matched a GPT-4o-mini controller’s accuracy with 75.5% fewer GPT tokens.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code not located

  6. CorePlans Change Answers

    When Plans Change Answers: Formalizing Cost-Accuracy Optimization for Semantic Queries

    Kyoungmin Kim

    Weighting each calibrated decision’s expected error by its contribution to the output predicts a plan’s output quality without labels; in simulation, plans for the same query differed by up to three orders of magnitude in cost, selection pushdown with bands calibrated on the plan’s own candidates met the output target on 62% of instances against 96% for pull-up, per-tuple prices met the target at about a fifth of the cost of the best operator-level band, and an overconfident model predicted output precision 0.981 where 0.872 was realised.

    arXiv preprint6 Oct 2026Code not located

  7. CoreCLM-as-a-Judge

    CLM-as-a-Judge: Evaluating an Open Contrastive Decision Model on Public Judge Benchmarks

    Gowthamkumar Nandakishore

    Zero-shot CLM scored 0.351–0.593 (at chance on RM-Bench and JudgeBench, one constant label on HaluEval) while a same-size reward model reached 0.764–0.976; raw confidences were overconfident by up to +0.401 but one pooled temperature brought ECE to at most 0.062, and order flips were 0.0002 against 0.2188 for the generative judge, yet a confidence-gated cascade escalated 92.3–100% of items to the strong judge.

    arXiv preprint5 Oct 2026CodePredictions

  8. CoreGraphDecide

    GraphDecide: Benchmarking System One Models on Graph Tasks

    Xianliang Yang, Yapu Zhang, Li Zhao

    Jev answered every adjacency query correctly and did well on cycle, connectivity and distance, yet reached only 67.80% on exact degree and 61.25% on articulation points; joint graph-text input did not consistently improve prediction, and feasible constructions did not imply good solutions.

    arXiv preprint5 Oct 2026CodePredictions (partial)

  9. CorePolitical Science Replications

    JEV versus LLMs: Accuracy, Cost and Calibration on Seven Political Science Replications

    Steven Denney, Matthew DiGiuseppe

    Jev matched or came close to both LLMs across tasks, but had no cost advantage over GPT-6 Luna at batch prices because it billed 1.2–3.3 times as many input tokens per decision; asked once, its probabilities were better calibrated than GPT-6 Luna’s token probabilities but not consistently better than Qwen3.8-27B’s.

    arXiv preprint5 Oct 2026Code not located

  10. CoreSharedKV-BT

    SharedKV-BT: Node-Local Typed Decisions for Behavior-Tree Agents

    Naoki Wake, Justin Wagle

    Exposing only the current node’s candidates raised correct joint typed decisions from 90/120 to 113/120 and closed-loop four-object completion from 0/30 to 18/30; prefix reuse alone cut decision latency 1.29–2.09-fold, and stage gating with external postconditions prevented out-of-order actions and premature completion, but an explicit ABSTAIN option rejected 28/30 invalid menus while choosing correctly in only 17/30 valid ones.

    arXiv preprint5 Oct 2026Code not located

  11. Coreufakzeka-karar

    ufakzeka-karar: An Open Turkish Typed-Decision Model with Order-Invariant Option Scoring

    Sait Furkan Teke

    Scoring options blind to each other made answers independent of order by construction, while a sequential head trained on shuffled options changed 2.3–2.8% of answers when only the order changed; REINFORCE lost 0.102 macro-F1 to cross-entropy. The released model ranks 7th of 16 on HakemBench (composite 0.660) at a median of 102.5 ms on one CPU thread, and temperature scaling raised held-out calibration error (0.027 → 0.045).

    arXiv preprint5 Oct 2026CodeWeights

  12. CoreFiorillo

    Calibrated Answers About Randomized Trials From a 4-Billion-Parameter Open Model: A Registered Test and a License-Clean Release

    Johann Emmanuel Li

    All four criteria registered before test predictions passed: ECE 0.0168 (limit 0.05), log loss below Gemma 4 31B-it by 0.1829 and macro-F1 0.9248 against 0.8668; reading checks showed macro-F1 0.4384 without the article, a gain from the title alone, and only 66.5% of direction answers reversed when intervention and comparator were exchanged.

    arXiv preprint4 Oct 2026CodeWeightsPredictions (partial)

  13. CoreCrystalJev

    CrystalJev: thinking fast and slow with atomistic foundation models for materials discovery

    Peng Kang, Zhen Li, Yu Liu et al.

    For MACE-MP-0 and CHGNet, one calibrated unrelaxed pass decided stability better than recalibrated relax-and-threshold (log-loss 0.191 against 0.247 and 0.196 against 0.266) at about a thirtieth of the cost, and slightly worse for four newer potentials; in a registered prospective test, single-pass forecasts calibrated only on existing data over-stated the stable fraction of unseen candidates (5.8%) by 0.8–2.1 points, with AUROC 0.965–0.982.

    arXiv preprint4 Oct 2026Code not located

  14. CoreHidden Risks of Jev

    Hidden Risks of Jev: An Empirical Study of Security, Privacy, and Dual Use

    Shang Wang, Tianqing Zhu, Huajie Chen et al.

    Context-ignoring injection reached 96.5% attack success against Jev on support routing and Jev-specific injections reached 100% on subscription cancellation and at least 96.8% on issue severity; backdoors implanted by poisoning NanoJev succeeded on over 89% of targeted inputs while clean accuracy stayed at 90.5–94.0%; as a detector Jev reached ROC-AUC 0.95–1.0 on all eight benchmarks, and the same capability supported jailbreak planning and model extraction.

    arXiv preprint4 Oct 2026Code

  15. CoreSearchJev

    SearchJev: A Fast and Calibrated System-1 Model for Search Agents

    Congfeng Cao, Lipeng Zuo, Konstantinos Papakostas et al.

    SearchJev beat same-size Qwen3.5 autoregressive models in decision quality while deciding 5.2–5.3 times faster with 41–74% lower average ECE; on held-out data it exceeded hosted Jev on query rewriting (and the 4B model on relevance) while Jev stayed stronger on routing and verification; in BrowseComp-Plus the dual-system agents cut median active search time 3.7–4.7-fold and raised answer accuracy from 45% to up to 54%.

    arXiv preprint4 Oct 2026CodeWeights

  16. CoreWireless Decision-Making

    System One Models for Wireless Decision-Making:Applications and Performance Evaluation

    Masoud Rahimi, S. M. Matin Alemohammad, Hamid Behroozi et al.

    In receive-antenna selection Jev cut median response latency by up to 8.5 times against the evaluated LLMs but lost decision quality to stronger task-specific methods; in intent-conditioned RAN slicing it reached utility comparable to the LLMs with more than 3.5 times lower median latency. Faster decisions did not necessarily mean lower end-to-end service latency.

    arXiv preprint3 Oct 2026Code not located

  17. CoreCandidate Coverage and Transfer

    Benchmarking candidate coverage and rejection policy transfer in typed decision models

    Jiawen Lu, Tongtong Wu

    Rejection thresholds calibrated on one task often failed on another: with 80 calibration pairs, none-score policies exceeded 5% target false rejection in six of twelve transfers for both Laya and Jev, a Jev policy fitted on DBpedia rejected 69.3% of covered Emotion inputs and an Emotion-fitted policy disabled rejection; with retrieved CLINC150 menus, more candidates raised Laya’s out-of-scope detection to 99.0% at 48.8% false rejection while Jev kept about 3% false rejection but detected 75.0%.

    arXiv preprint2 Oct 2026CodePredictions

  18. CoreJEVal and InnerJev

    General Decision Models: Benchmarking and Insights Beyond Jev

    Feiyu Duan, Jiayu Lin, Jia Wang et al.

    The strongest decision models came within three points of leading thinking LLMs overall but fell behind on specialist knowledge; Jev picked the mode on all 100 prior-probability items yet put 89.35% of the mass on it against 35.60% in the true distribution; in τ-bench, Jev action selection cut median episode time from 267 s to 161 s but lowered success from 77.6% to 66.1%; InnerJev-27B performed on par with Jev at about 0.1 s per query.

  19. CoreSecJev

    SecJev: Bringing Security Expertise to System One Decision Models

    Zheng Chen, Fei Yu, Haohao Huang et al.

    Security specialisation improved every model in the family: SecJev-0.8B gained 39.40 points of task-macro accuracy over Kev-0.8B and beat general Kev-9B by 20.51 points, with accuracy and latency close to answer-only generative fine-tuning at lower peak memory; gains over Kev held on new prompt-injection and traffic sources, with capture-dependent false alarms.

10 About

How the evidence was gathered, and how to cite it

What counts as a study here, how each one is read, related reviews, release notes and the data behind every section.

Scope & sources

  • Core studies evaluate Jev, build a Jev-like typed decision model, or depend on one inside a system.
  • Background references cover adjacent and earlier work: classifiers, calibration, selective prediction, structured output, routing, judging and related reviews.
  • Studies come from repeated arXiv searches; repositories from GitHub searches and curated lists, each checked against its own README. Queries and screening decisions are published with the data.

Reading the evidence

  • Core studies are read in full, and revised versions are re-read; every evidence record points to the section or table it comes from.
  • Values are as reported by authors, the vendor or community repositories; they were not re-run.
  • Openness is recorded field by field — code, weights, data, predictions — because each can be released on its own.
