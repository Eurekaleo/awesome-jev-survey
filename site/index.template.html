Jev Survey

Every answer type-checks. The evidence decides the rest.

TypeSafe’s Jev returns typed answers with a probability for every option, and open projects now copy its shape. This survey reads the studies, audits the open resources and traces every number to its source: what the probabilities mean, when software should act on them, and where typed decisions fail.

02 The object

A function call that returns a distribution

Jev is a hosted model from TypeSafe AI, released on 15 September 2026 as its first “System One” model. Software sends a text state and typed questions; the model answers from the declared options with probabilities and never writes free text.

{{primitives}}
A

The commercial model

TypeSafe’s hosted Jev, version jev-1.13.0. Its interface, prices, limits and known weak spots are documented; its architecture, training data and confidence statistic are not.

Evidence: black-box evaluations

B

The paradigm

Jev-like typed decision models: encoders, encoder–decoders, frozen or fine-tuned decoders, diffusion reads and adapters that reproduce the request and response shape with other mechanisms.

Evidence: open code and weights, of uneven completeness

C

The systems

Judges, agents, search engines, databases, robots and annotation pipelines whose results depend on typed decisions — and on much else.

Evidence: end-to-end outcomes that need attribution

Documented by the vendor

  • Input is text only: a string, JSON object or array of text. Images, audio and video must be converted first.
  • Versioned IDs; the aliases jev-latest and jev-preview point to jev-1.13.0.
  • confidence is a statistic of the returned distribution; yes/no answers carry none.
  • Calibration is measured over groups of predictions and does not certify an individual answer.
  • A published list of known weak spots ↗: literal reading, math and numbers, dates, indirection, large states, adversarial text and Choice option order.

Vendor-reported, not independently tested

03 The map

Five places a claim can live

Each study is placed where its claim sits on the path from a declared question to a workflow outcome. The map is a frame for comparing evidence, not an architecture.

    {{stages}}

Six research questions

RQ1

Definition & lineage

How do runtime-defined finite-option decisions relate to classifiers, entailment-based labelling, rerankers, reward models and constrained generation?

RQ2

Probability quality

What do returned probabilities and confidence mean, for which tasks, languages and distributions, and are they coherent across related questions?

RQ3

Efficiency attribution

How much of a speed or cost gain comes from the readout, and how much from model size, batching, prefix sharing, caching, pruning or the network?

RQ4

Selective control

Which decisions can be delegated, and when should software escalate, abstain or let code compute — judged by end outcome and total cost?

RQ5

Robustness & security

How do option names, order, definitions, missing options, language, injected text and poisoned checkpoints change decisions?

RQ6

Openness & reproducibility

What do alternatives release — code, weights, data, predictions — and how much of the literature survives a common protocol?

06 Applications & selective control

A first pass, not a replacement

Across domains the typed model is usually the cheapest and fastest component and rarely the most accurate. Reported gains come from routing uncertain cases elsewhere, with thresholds fixed on separate data, and from letting code compute what the model would only estimate.
